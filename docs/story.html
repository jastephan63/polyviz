Anatomy of a Swiss canton

Canton Lucerne counted 442,119 people at the end of 2025, and its statistical office opens enough data to take the place apart properly. This page asks four questions of it. How many people will the canton hold, and how old will they be? Where does the growth land, and what arrives with it? Do poor municipalities catch up with rich ones — the oldest question in regional economics — or drift further behind? And how much of any of this could you have predicted from what is visible today? The answers come from LUSTAT Statistik Luzern's open municipal data and the Bundesamt für Statistik's federal series, fetched live when this page is built. Every chart is interactive — hover, and take any of them along via the download button in its top-right corner — and every number in the text is computed from the data at build time, none typed in from memory. The methods, and their limits, are laid out in the appendix.

Drei Zukünfte — three futures

LUSTAT projects every municipality's population to 2055, three ways, one year and one year of age at a time. The question this chapter asks of the scenarios is not how big the canton gets — all three agree it grows — but what the growth is made of, and which parts of the future no scenario can bend.

Three roads out of the present

The canton counts 442,119 permanent residents at the end of 2025. LUSTAT's reference scenario carries that to 581,571 by 2055 — 31.5% more people — and the weaker and stronger variants bracket the endpoint between 506,711 and 656,241, a spread of 149,530 residents. Note what the fan does not contain: a future in which the canton stops growing. Even the cautious path adds 64,592 people, and on the reference path the canton passes the half-million mark around 2038. The real uncertainty, the next three charts argue, is not whether Lucerne grows but what the growth is made of — and what it cannot change.

What the growth is made of

Decompose the reference path into its three flows and the growth changes character. Over the thirty years the canton gains 132,667 people from net migration and only 11,434 from natural change — 92% of the net gain walks in from outside. And the natural surplus is a closing scissors: births rise from 4,251 to 5,810 a year, but deaths rise faster, from 3,499 to 5,835, until they cross in 2055. The weaker variant crosses already in 2028; the stronger one never inside the horizon. Whichever line the canton follows, its growth engine is migration — a fact that matters for everything the later chapters find about where growth lands.

The ratio no scenario can bend

Here is the sharpest finding in the scenario data. The old-age dependency ratio — residents 65 and older per 100 of working age — climbs from 31.9 to 41.9 on the reference path, a rise of a third. Now compare the scenarios: in 2055 they end within 1.3 points of each other, while the population totals they belong to differ by 25.7%. Thirty years of higher or lower migration moves the size of the canton a great deal and its age structure almost not at all — the ageing wave is baked into cohorts already born. The youth ratio barely moves either (33.3 to 33.9 young people per 100 of working age), so the whole shift lands on the old-age side of the ledger.

The ageing wave

The wave the dependency ratio measures is visible cohort by cohort. In 2025, 19.3% of residents are 65 or older; by 2055 it is 23.8%. The 80-plus group grows fastest of all, from 24,470 to 49,893 people — 2.0 times as many — the band thickening at the top of the chart. The strongest diagonal is the canton's largest cohort, the roughly 35-year-olds of 2025 (born around 1990), climbing one row every five years; follow it and it enters retirement age in the 2050s. Nothing migration can plausibly do dissolves that diagonal — it can only pour younger rows in beneath it.

Wo das Wachstum landet — where the growth lands

A canton does not grow in the aggregate; particular houses get built in particular villages. This chapter drops the projected growth onto the map and asks which municipalities carry it, whether it arrives in cradles or moving vans, and why the demand for homes grows faster than the count of people.

Where the growth lands

The reference scenario adds 139,452 residents to the canton — 31.5% — but drops them very unevenly on the map. Emmen grows fastest, by 75.7%, with Kriens (73.6%) and Buchrain (67.7%) behind it; at the other end Escholzmatt-Marbach is projected to shrink by 15.1%. Only 12 of the 79 municipalities out-grow the cantonal average, and 14 are projected to shrink. The growth is concentrated, not sprinkled — and the concentration has a shape the clustering in chapter four will make precise: a corridor around the lake, a belt that keeps pace, and a hinterland that empties.

Who carries the growth

Sorting the same 79 futures by today's size shows who carries the growth. The median municipality under 2,000 residents grows 6.6%, the mid-sized ones 17.5%, and the 7 municipalities over 10,000 grow 50.4% at the median — the canton's future is being built where people already are. The capital itself (87,384 residents in 2025) sits at 27.4%, below the canton-wide 31.5% — the city region grows through the ring around the city, not the city alone.

Cradles or moving vans

Chapter one showed the canton's growth is imported; this chart shows the same is true municipality by municipality. Each dot places a municipality by its two cumulative flows over the thirty years, as a share of its 2025 population. 28 of the 79 municipalities sit in the upper-left quadrant — more deaths than births, growth only if the moving vans keep coming — and 42 more grow on both counts. Just 9 lose people on both. The extremes tell the story: Kriens tops the migration axis at 67% of its base population, while Eich runs the deepest natural deficit at -15%; the largest natural surplus, 16%, belongs to Emmen — the young families are already where the growth corridor is.

The households it arrives in

Growth is usually counted in people, but it lands in households — and those are a different series. Between 2012 and 2024 the canton's household count grew 19.6% while the people living in them grew roughly 14.0%: the average household thinned from about 2.33 to 2.22 persons (counting six-plus households as six, so the true figures sit a shade higher). One- and two-person households now make up 69.2% of all households, up from 65.7%. Every percentage point of population growth therefore demands more than a point of new dwellings — the quiet multiplier underneath every housing debate the coming 139,452 residents will trigger.

Ungleiche Kassen — unequal coffers

Do poor municipalities catch up with rich ones? Growth theory says they should; this chapter tests it on eight years of Lucerne's equalisation accounts — the classic convergence regressions first, then the concentration of the tax base, what the equalisation transfers actually achieve against it, and what fiscal weakness costs the people who live in it.

The fiscal map

The tax base is as unevenly spread as the growth. In the 2027 equalisation year, Meggen tops the resource index at 285.6 — 2.9 times the cantonal average of 100 — while Romoos sits at 37.2. 65 of the 78 municipalities fall below the average, and the resource equalisation moves CHF 156 million to 54 of them. The interesting question is not the snapshot, though: it is whether the gap closes over time. The textbook says capital and people flowing to cheap land should pull the laggards up. The next four charts test that story on eight years of equalisation accounts.

The catch-up that isn't happening

The classic convergence test regresses growth on the starting level: if poor municipalities catch up, the line slopes down. It does not. Across the 78 municipalities with a full record, a hundred extra points of initial resource index go with 0.36 percentage points of annual growth (95% CI -0.38 to 1.09) — a confidence band that straddles zero comfortably — and the starting level explains 1.2% of the variation in growth (R² = 0.012). With n = 78 and 7 years this is an association test, not a verdict on causes, and it cannot rule out slow convergence hiding under noise. But the textbook's strong claim — poor places growing systematically faster — simply is not in these accounts.

The spread, measured twice

Sigma-convergence asks the same question without a regression: is the dispersion of per-capita resources shrinking? Both measures say no. The coefficient of variation rises from 0.42 to 0.45 over the eight equalisation years (+7%), the standard deviation of log resources from 0.34 to 0.35. Neither line moves dramatically, and neither moves in a straight line — both dip and wobble on the way — but both end higher than they began: the municipalities are drifting apart, not together. One movement underneath is steady, though: the share of the resource potential held by the weakest half of the canton's residents slips from 36.7% to 32.9% across the same years. The next two charts measure that concentration properly — and what the transfers achieve against it.

How far the curve bends

The Lorenz curve makes the concentration measurable. Sort residents by their municipality's per-capita resources and walk up the distribution: in the 2027 equalisation year, the half of the canton's residents in the weakest municipalities command 32.9% of the resource potential, while the strongest tenth holds 17.9%. The bend of the curve is the Gini coefficient — 0.228 before equalisation. The transfers flatten it to 0.158: real compression, achieved with about CHF 156 million, though the curve never comes close to the diagonal. This is a floor under the weak, not a ceiling on the strong.

Running to stand still

Put the whole period on one chart and the two findings separate cleanly. The upper line is concentration as the tax bases produce it: the Gini climbs from 0.187 to 0.228 — 22% higher in 2027 than in 2020, rising in nearly every year. There is no turning point to annotate; the climb is the story. The lower line is the same coefficient after the resource transfers, which strip out roughly 30% of the inequality year after year (0.133 falling to 0.158 means the after-line climbs too). Equalisation, in other words, is running to stand still: it compresses each year's gap while the underlying gap widens underneath it.

The price of a weak tax base

What does fiscal weakness cost the people who live in it? The tax multiplier answers: municipalities set their own, and the correlation with the resource index is r = -0.80 — among the tightest relationships in this story (R² = 0.63). Along the fitted line, a hundred additional index points go with a multiplier about 0.64 units lower. At the extremes, Meggen taxes at 0.93 units on an index of 285.6 while Luthern charges 2.40 on 40.5. The direction of causation runs both ways — weak bases force high rates, and high rates repel the mobile tax base — which is precisely why the equalisation of the previous charts exists. The map, the Gini, and this line are one phenomenon seen three ways.

Familien von Gemeinden — families of municipalities

Eighty municipal stories are too many to hold in the head, so this chapter lets the data sort them: every municipality becomes ten standardised numbers, Ward's method builds the family tree, and the map and the profiles say what makes each family a family — and whether the families the algorithm finds are the regions the earlier chapters kept meeting.

The family tree

Rather than assert that the canton has 'types' of municipality, let the data propose them. Each of the 78 municipalities becomes a point in ten dimensions — size, projected and recent growth, age, foreign share, both projected flows, fiscal strength, tax multiplier, and one-person-household share, each standardised — and Ward's method merges the closest pairs upward into this tree. Cutting it at four families of 13, 51, 11, and 3 municipalities is a judgement call the appendix defends honestly: a plain silhouette score prefers the coarser two-way cut (0.39 vs 0.23 at k = 4), but the two extra families the k = 4 cut separates — the emptying hinterland and the lakeshore enclave — are exactly the structures the earlier chapters kept meeting. The tree shows both cuts; the colours mark the one we read.

The families on the map

The clustering used no geography — no coordinates, no distances, no neighbourhoods — yet painted on the map, the families assemble into almost contiguous regions: the 13 hinterland municipalities fill the rural south-west around the Entlebuch, the 51-strong middle belt wraps around them, the 11 corridor municipalities trace the agglomeration around the capital and the axis out toward Sursee, and the 3 lakeshore communities sit together on the lake's northern shore. That geography emerges from demography and money alone is the point: place in this canton is destiny enough that ten statistical features recover the map. (78 of 79 municipalities carry all ten features; any without a complete record stays grey.)

What makes each family a family

Reading the columns names the families. Family 1 (13 municipalities, mean 2,065 residents) is the emptying hinterland: resource index 55.8, the canton's highest tax multipliers, projected change -7.1%. Family 2 (51 municipalities) is the quiet middle — near the cantonal average on almost every row, which is what makes it a family. Family 3 (11 municipalities, mean 19,388 residents) is the growth corridor: the urban belt, 56.8% projected growth, a foreign share of 26.1% against the hinterland's 8.2%. And family 4 (3 municipalities — Meggen, Vitznau, Weggis) is lakeshore wealth: resource index 232.4, multiplier 1.23, the oldest population and the most one-person households — rich, grey, and slow-growing by design.

Was sich wissen lässt — what can be known

A projection is a claim about the future, and claims can be interrogated: what is this one made of? This chapter runs three honest prediction exercises — each scored out of sample, each reported with the boring baseline it must beat — and finds that the projected map is mostly today's structure, that momentum alone is a poor guide, and that one future in this story is genuinely unpredictable.

Momentum is not the model

A cheap theory of any projection is that it extrapolates the recent past. Test it: the equalisation accounts carry each municipality's actual population base (a three-year average that trails the calendar by three to five years), so its recent growth — roughly 2016 to 2023 — can be set against the projected rate. The correlation is r = 0.37: recent momentum explains 14% of the variation in the projected map (R² = 0.14). Momentum matters, but LUSTAT's model is clearly drawing on more than yesterday's trend — zoning capacity, age structure, planned development. What that 'more' consists of, the next chart reverse-engineers.

Reverse-engineering the projection

How much of the scenario's municipal geography can be recovered from facts observable today? Feed seven present-day features — size, age, foreign share, fiscal strength, tax multiplier, recent growth, household structure — into a linear model, and score it honestly: each municipality is predicted by a model fitted to the other 77 (leave-one-out cross-validation). The cross-validated error is 13.0 growth points, against 20.5 for simply guessing the cantonal mean — 37% smaller. In-sample the model explains 69% of the variance. The scenario's geography, in other words, is largely today's structure projected forward, which is reassuring about the model and sobering about the hinterland: nothing in today's observables points its way up.

The unpredictable part

Run the same honest machinery on a real future — which tax bases grew fastest over 2020–2027 — and it collapses. The six features manage a leave-one-out error of 1.27 points of annual growth; guessing the cantonal mean for every municipality scores 1.18. The model is worse than knowing nothing — the signature of a regression fitting noise (even in-sample, R² reaches only 0.04). This is the finding, not a failure: municipal fiscal fortunes over a few years ride on firm relocations, property deals, and a handful of big taxpayers — none of it visible in structural statistics. Where chapter three found no convergence, this chart explains why no simple story fits: year-to-year fiscal change is, to these features, noise.

Der Kanton im Land — the canton in the country

None of this happens on an island. Three federal series place Lucerne in the Swiss frame: the capital's rank among the cities, the growth engine every city runs on, and the sector that already dominates the capital's economy just as the ageing wave heads its way.

The capital on the national ladder

Zoom out to the whole country and the cantonal capital holds a remarkably steady place: rank 8 among Switzerland's statistical cities in 1930, rank 7 in 2024. The path between was anything but steady — the city grew to 83,374 residents by 1970, lent 7,949 of them to its suburbs by 2000, and only passed its 1970 size again in 2024, at 86,234 residents. The arc is the national rule, not an exception: 6 of the eight cities on this ladder trace the same hollowing-out, and only 3 have yet climbed back past their old peak. It is the growth corridor of chapter four in national miniature: the region grows while the core city merely persists.

Every city's growth engine

Lucerne's migration-driven growth is not a local quirk. Across the 180 Swiss statistical cities in 2024, 72 — 40% — already record more deaths than births; whatever growth they still manage arrives by van. The capital sits at 0.9 per 1,000 on the natural axis and 7.1 per 1,000 on migration: a modest natural surplus topped up several times over by arrivals. The pattern chapter one projected for the canton's future is the present tense of urban Switzerland — the scenario models are not forecasting something exotic, they are spreading something already here.

What the capital does for a living

One more national comparison rounds out the ageing story. 21.6% of the city of Luzern's employees work in health and social work, 2.2 times the share of the next sector, trade & repair (9.7%); only 30 of the 180 Swiss statistical cities lean harder on health. An economy already organised around care meets a canton whose 80-plus population, the first chapter computed, will multiply by 2.0 — the capital's biggest sector is the one demographic arithmetic most guarantees.

Methoden und Daten — methods and data

What was computed, how, and what it cannot show. Shorter than a journal's methods section, longer than a caption — enough to re-run every number on this page.

Data

Everything on this page comes from openly licensed Swiss statistics, fetched at build time through the package's own fetchers (pv_fetch_lustat(), pv_fetch_bfs()) and cached locally, so the build re-runs offline. LUSTAT files carry the opendata.swiss OPEN BY ASK terms, stats.swiss files OPEN BY; the exact terms print on every fetch. Every number in the prose is computed in data-raw/build-story.R from these files at build time — none is typed in from knowledge.

Convergence (chapter 3)

Beta: annualised growth of per-capita resource potential 2020–2027 regressed on the initial resource index, for the 78 municipalities present in both years (4 dropped to mergers). Slope 0.36 points of annual growth per 100 index points, 95% CI [-0.38, 1.09], R² = 0.012. Sigma: the coefficient of variation and the s.d. of log per-capita resources, per year, unweighted across municipalities. Both are association tests on a short window with n ≈ 78; they rule out visible convergence in this period, nothing stronger.

Inequality (chapter 3)

Gini coefficients are population-weighted: municipalities are sorted by per-capita resource potential, residents accumulate along the x-axis using the equalisation file's own population base (mwb_bj3_bj5), and the Gini is one minus twice the area under the Lorenz curve. 'After equalisation' adds each municipality's resource-equalisation transfer, divided by the same population, to its per-capita resources — an approximation that ignores the burden-sharing instruments, so it understates the full system's compression. 2027 values: before 0.228, after 0.158.

Families (chapter 4)

Ward clustering (hclust, ward.D2, Euclidean) on ten standardised features, for the 78 of the canton's 79 municipalities with a complete fiscal history and household record. Mean silhouette: 0.39 (k = 2), 0.38 (k = 3), 0.23 (k = 4), 0.22 (k = 5). The silhouette prefers k = 2; we cut at k = 4 because the two additional families are interpretable, geographically coherent, and match structures found independently in chapters 2 and 3 — a documented judgement, not a statistic.

Prediction (chapter 5)

Linear models scored by leave-one-out cross-validation: each municipality is predicted by a model fitted to all others, and the RMSE of those held-out predictions is compared with the RMSE of guessing the (held-out) mean. Projected growth: RMSE 13.0 vs 20.5 (37% better). Actual fiscal growth: RMSE 1.27 vs 1.18 — the model loses, which is reported as the finding, not smoothed over; even before cross-validation its in-sample R² reaches only 0.04.

Limits

The population scenarios are LUSTAT's model output, not observations — chapters 1, 2, and 5 analyse what the model asserts, and say so. The equalisation file's population base is a lagged three-year average, so 'recent growth' spans roughly 2016–2023. With ~79 municipalities every regression is small-sample; confidence intervals are reported where a claim depends on them. Associations are never read as causes. And one dataset hunt failed honestly: municipal-level historical population, dwellings, and vacancy series exist on stats.swiss but only as full-table downloads too large for this build, so the historical record here rests on the equalisation base and the household statistics.
